📚 Stage 2: Vocabulary▲

❤️ Core Concepts & Feelings

Casualties
the individuals or ruined relationships resulting from destructive interpersonal conflict.
Both partners felt like casualties of war after months of constant arguing.
Conflict
a serious, ongoing disagreement or emotional battle between partners.
In a bitter conflict, even the winner suffers severe emotional damage.
Battles
individual arguments or power struggles within a romantic partnership.
You might win individual battles, but still end up destroying the entire relationship.
Scars
lasting emotional wounds left behind after a painful romantic breakup.
Long after the relationship ended, the psychological scars remained.
Score-keeping
the destructive habit of continuously tracking past mistakes and grievances against a partner.
Her habit of keeping score made it impossible to forgive past mistakes.

🗣️ Life, Actions & Connection

Regret
a feeling of sadness or remorse over past choices, such as staying too long in a toxic relation.
Looking back, she felt deep regret that she hadn't walked away much sooner.
Vulnerability
the state of being exposed to emotional hurt when dropping one's defensive armor.
Fighting back tears in front of her ex was a moment of painful vulnerability.
Opposite: Defensiveness
Broken dreams
unfulfilled plans, promises, and future expectations that collapse during a breakup.
Hearing the sound of broken dreams hitting the ground made the breakup feel final.
Lost cause
a relationship or situation that is beyond saving despite all efforts.
Trying to repair the trust was a lost cause once resentment took over.
Fallout
the emotional after-effects and collateral damage following a major conflict or breakup.
The emotional fallout of their argument affected all their mutual friends.

✨ Stage 5: Language Focus▲

Language Focus: Past Modals of Relationship Regret (*could have / should have / might have*)

In the song Casualties of War, the lines "I could've walked away, I should've walked away" and "You might have won the battle but not the war" demonstrate Past Modals (*could have / should have / might have + past participle*) used to analyze past romantic mistakes, missed exit points, and pyrrhic victories.

Let's Practise — Three Examples:
  • I should have walked away before the argument turned hurtful.
  • We could have resolved our differences if we had communicated with less defensiveness.
  • He might have won the debate, but he lost her trust in the process.
🎭 Stage 6: Final Challenge▲
🎭

Relationship Post-Mortem Role-Play: Work in pairs. Role-play a constructive conversation between two ex-partners meeting six months after a painful breakup. Practice expressing past regrets using past modals (should have / could have) while establishing healthy boundaries and mutual forgiveness.

✏️ Teacher's Note (Linguistic Corrections)▲
I should walk away when the argument started. → I should have walked away when the argument started. (Use 'should have + past participle' to express regret about past actions.)
We are casualties from this argument. → We are casualties of this argument. (Use the preposition 'of' with 'casualties' in this idiom.)
She is habituated to keep score. → She has a habit of keeping score. (Use 'has a habit of + gerund' or 'is in the habit of'.)

Conditionals and Trust with 'I trust you'

In English, using 'I trust you' with conditional clauses starting with 'if' structures agreements and expectations between two people.

Expressing Modals of Possibility ('might / would')

In English, 'might' expresses a possibility or risk in the present or future, while 'would' or 'would rather' expresses preference or hypothethicals.

  • Romance might be a blur, but our connection feels very real.

The Modal Verb 'Must' for Obligation and Deduction

We use 'must' to express a strong obligation or a logical deduction directly from the lyrics.

Let's Practise — Three Examples:

  • The song is so beautiful, she must be a great artist.
  • You must listen carefully to fill in the missing gaps.
