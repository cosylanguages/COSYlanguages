Quantifiers & Countability

Countable vs uncountable nouns, some/any, and much/many/a lot of

🎯 What you can communicate after this page: You can express quantities of food, money, time, and objects accurately in positive, negative, and question forms.

💡 Check your understanding (CCQs)

Answer these quick questions to verify meaning:

1. Sentence: "Countable (apples, cars)" — Is staying inside dependent on the condition of rain?
Conditionals state that an outcome depends on whether the 'if' clause occurs.
2. Sentence: "Countable (apples, cars)" — If it does NOT rain, does the condition apply?
The outcome is contingent on the 'if' condition.

👀 What do you notice?

Notice: Countable nouns can be counted (1 apple, 2 apples). Uncountable nouns cannot be counted individually (water, rice, money).

Noun TypePositive (+)Negative (-) & Question (?)
Countable (apples, cars)some apples / a lot of carsany apples / many cars
Uncountable (water, money)some water / a lot of moneyany water / much money

🧠 Think like a native speaker

In positive statements, native speakers prefer 'a lot of' for both countable and uncountable nouns instead of 'much' or 'many'. 'I have a lot of money' sounds much more natural than 'I have much money'.

🗣️ Pronunciation Note

'Some' in relaxed speech is reduced to /səm/ ('some water'). 'A lot of' connects as /ə.lɒt.əv/.

Key examples
  • There is some cheese in the fridge.
  • We don't have any butter left.
  • How many chairs do we need?

💡 Memory trick

SOME for Positive statements. ANY for Negatives & Questions. MANY for items you count, MUCH for stuff you measure!
