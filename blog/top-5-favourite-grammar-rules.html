My Top 5 Favourite English Grammar Rules for Intermediate Learners

J Written by JY DM · 📅 Published 2026-09-15 · ⏱️ 6 min read
🎧 Page Audio Guide & Narration Page 1 of 7

For many language learners, grammar feels like a strict set of rules designed to catch mistakes. Traditional textbooks often focus on rigid formulas: subject plus verb plus object, strict tense charts, and endless lists of exceptions.

However, when you reach the intermediate level (CEFR B1–B2), grammar transforms into something much more exciting: a creative toolkit for expressing emotion, playfulness, and social connection. Native speakers constantly adapt and stretch grammar patterns to communicate subtle shades of meaning.

Here are my top 5 favourite grammar rules in English that go beyond standard textbook formulas and give your intermediate English immediate natural flair.

1. The Softening Power of the -ish Suffix

In formal English, precision is valued. But in everyday casual speech, sounding too precise can sometimes feel stiff or overly formal. That is where the magical suffix -ish comes in.

Adding -ish to numbers, times, colors, and adjectives signals approximation, flexibility, or polite hesitation:

  • Approximate Time & Numbers:
    • "Let's meet at seven-ish." (Around 7:00, perhaps 7:05 or 7:10)
    • "He looks thirty-ish." (Approximately thirty years old)
  • Colors & Qualities:
    • "She wore a greenish sweater." (Not purely green, but close)
    • "The movie was long-ish, but good." (Somewhat long)
  • Softening Opinions & States:
    • "Are you hungry?" "Hungry-ish!" (A little bit hungry)
    • "Was he upset?" "He seemed annoyed-ish."

Using -ish allows you to sound relaxed and conversational without having to commit to absolute precision.

2. Emotional -ing with Stative Verbs

Standard textbook grammar teaches a clear rule: stative verbs (such as love, hate, like, understand, want) describe permanent states and should never be used in the present continuous tense (-ing).

While that rule holds true for formal academic writing, native speakers routinely break it in spoken English to convey heightened, temporary emotion:

  • Present Enjoyment: "I'm loving this new podcast!" (I am actively experiencing great joy right now, rather than just stating a general fact)
  • Temporary Dislike: "I'm hating this rain today." (A strong, immediate emotional reaction to the weather right now)
  • Checking In: "Are you liking the food?" (Asking about your real-time experience at this moment)
  • Dynamic Realization: "I'm understanding grammar much better these days!" (Highlighting an ongoing process of growth)

Shifting a stative verb into the continuous form adds warmth, vivid energy, and immediacy to your expression.

3. The Functional Multi-Tool: "Work is Working" / "Work is Not Working"

The verb work is one of the most powerful and versatile words in intermediate English. Beginners usually learn work strictly as "having a job" or "doing tasks at an office." But its true communicative power lies in its broader meanings: functioning, effectiveness, and suitability.

Meaning Example Sentence
Functioning / Operating "The elevator isn't working today."
Producing Desired Results "Our study strategy is really working!"
Convenient / Suitable "Does Thursday at 3 PM work for you?"
Job & Productivity "I have a lot of work to complete."

This gives rise to the clever dual expression: "Work is working" (My job/project is producing great results) versus "Work is not working" (My current job routine or strategy is failing). Understanding these layers makes your English instantly more flexible.

4. Verb Shifting (Turning Nouns into Active Verbs)

English is famous for a process known as zero-derivation or verbing: taking a well-known noun and turning it directly into a verb without changing its form.

This rule allows English speakers to create vivid, economical verbs on the fly to describe daily habits, modern culture, and social routines:

  • Social Activities:
    • "Let's coffee tomorrow morning." (Instead of: "Let's go drink coffee together")
    • "We were desk-lunching all week." (Eating lunch at one's desk while continuing to work)
  • Modern Responsibilities & Tech:
    • "I am adulting today by organizing my taxes." (Performing adult duties)
    • "Just Google the location." (Searching on Google)
    • "She bookmarked the article for later."

Verb shifting makes your language feel contemporary, dynamic, and direct.

5. Modern Vibe Evaluators: "It's giving..."

Language evolves rapidly, especially online and among younger generations. One of the most widespread modern grammar frames for evaluating aesthetics, moods, or attitudes is "It's giving...".

This structure functions as a quick comparative shorthand meaning "It radiates the vibe or energy of...":

  • Evaluating Aesthetic & Vibe:
    • "It's giving main character energy." (It exudes confidence and spotlight presence)
    • "This cafe is giving 90s nostalgia." (It feels reminiscent of the 1990s)
  • Evaluating Quality & Atmosphere:
    • "That outfit is giving expensive." (It looks high-end and luxurious)
    • "The presentation is giving chaotic." (It feels messy or disorganized)

Understanding "it's giving..." helps you decode informal commentary across podcasts, social media, and modern conversations effortlessly.

Summary Practice: Combining All 5 Rules

Notice how applying these 5 grammar rules upgrades a rigid textbook paragraph into natural, expressive B2 conversational English:

Rigid Textbook B1 Version:
"Let us meet around seven o'clock. I am enjoying this restaurant very much. My new business plan is producing good results. We are eating lunch at our desks now. This decor looks like a luxury hotel."

Natural Expressive B2 Version:
"Let's meet at seven-ish if that works for you! I'm loving this new spot. The new strategy is definitely working, and while we're desk-lunching today, notice how this place is giving luxury resort."

Mastering grammar isn't about avoiding mistakes: it is about choosing the right linguistic tool to make your voice memorable!
