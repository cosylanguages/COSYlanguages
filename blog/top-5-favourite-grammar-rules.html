Page 1: The Birthday Editorial & Stepping into Shakespeare's Creative Laboratory 📜

"October is my absolute favourite month — not only because it is my birthday month, but because autumn turns the world into a poet's studio. When intermediate learners ask me how to break through the B1–B2 plateau, my answer is simple: step into the shoes of William Shakespeare." — JY DM

Many learners do not realize that William Shakespeare was the ultimate language innovator. He did not merely follow grammar formulas; he coined over 1,700 new words — including everyday terms like fashionable, uncomfortable, manager, lonely, and elbow — by turning nouns into verbs and adding expressive suffixes.

At the intermediate level (CEFR B1–B2), true fluency begins when you embrace this same creative mindset. Grammar is not a static list of rules to fear, but a dynamic canvas for self-expression. In this October Birthday Special Edition, we explore my Top 5 Favourite English Grammar Rules — featuring CELTA concept checks on every single page!

Page 2: Rule 1 — The Softening Magic of -ish & The Suffix Atelier (-like, -esque) 🍂

1. Core Meaning: Standard Nationalities

The suffix -ish traditionally designates origin or belonging to a nation or group.

  • Spanish — "She speaks fluent Spanish and enjoys Spanish guitar music."
  • English — "English grammar offers creative conversational flexibility."
  • Danish — "Danish furniture is famous for elegant minimalism."
  • Finnish — "Finnish education emphasizes play and pupil well-being."

2. Special Usage: Time, Age, Behaviour & Modifiers

In modern spoken English, -ish serves as a versatile conversational softener:

  • a) Time & Age (Approximation):
    • Formal: "Let us meet at exactly 7:00 PM." vs Informal: "Let's meet at seven-ish."
    • Formal: "He appears to be thirty years of age." vs Informal: "He looks thirty-ish."
  • b) Behaviour & Personality:
    • Well-known: childish, stylish, foolish.
    • Creative modern coinages: babyish, introvert-ish, adult-ish.
  • c) Prepositions & Modifiers:
    • Formal: "The venue is near the central station." vs Informal: "It's near-ish to the station."

3. Suffix Atelier: -like & -esque

  • -like (Resemblance / Character): adult-like, baby-like, cat-like.
  • -esque (Artistic & Atmospheric Flair): van-Gogh-esque, Shakespeare-esque, Kafka-esque.

Page 3: Rule 2 — Emotional -ing: Giving Stative Feelings a Spoken Stage ☕

1. Meaning: Stative vs Dynamic Emotion Shift

Traditional textbooks state stative verbs (love, hate, like, understand, want) describe permanent states and cannot take the -ing continuous form. In spoken English, shifting stative verbs into continuous forms signals real-time emotion.

2. Special Usage: Real-Time Emotion & Check-ins

  • Present Emotion: "I'm loving this hot autumn tea!" (Heightened live joy)
  • Temporary Dislike: "I'm hating this rain today." (Immediate reaction)
  • Social Check-in: "Are you liking the exhibition?" (Asking about live experience)

Page 4: Rule 3 — Action Verbing: "Coffeeing", "Machining" & "Braining" ☕⚙️

1. Meaning: Turning Nouns into Active State Verbs

Modern spoken English uses an ultra-productive pattern: taking any noun or machine and turning it into an active continuous verb ([Noun] is [Noun]-ing) to express whether an object, person, or brain is fulfilling its purpose.

2. Special Usage: "Coffeeing", "Machining" & "Braining"

  • Work & Effort: "Work is working!" (Great results) vs "Work is not working!" (Workflow failure).
  • Coffee & Energy: "Coffee is coffee-ing today!" (Great focus) vs "Coffee is not coffee-ing..." (Still tired).
  • Technology & Mind: "The machine is not machining today." / "My brain is not braining."

Page 5: Rule 4 — Zero-Derivation Verb Shifting: From Shakespeare's Lexicon to C1 Exams 💻

1. Meaning: Zero-Derivation ("Verbing")

English converts well-known nouns directly into active verbs without changing forms, creating concise, high-frequency verbs. Shakespeare famously used zero-derivation to turn elbow into to elbow and fool into to fool!

2. Special Usage: Social Habits & Modern Work

  • Social Habits: "Let's coffee tomorrow morning."
  • Work Routine: "We're desk-lunching all week."
  • Modern Responsibilities: "I am adulting today by organizing my tax documents."

3. High-Scoring Academic Verbs for Language Exams 📝

  • to benchmark — "The report benchmarks current environmental standards."
  • to spearhead — "The organization spearheaded a new initiative."
  • to bottleneck — "Supply chain issues bottlenecked production."

Page 6: Rule 5 — Modern Vibe Evaluators: "It's Giving..." (The Shakespearean Aesthetic) 🎃

1. Meaning: Aesthetic & Mood Evaluation

The popular spoken frame "It's giving..." functions as shorthand for "It radiates the aura, energy, or aesthetic of...".

2. Special Usage: Aesthetic & Atmosphere

  • Atmosphere: "This autumn cafe is giving cozy October vibes."
  • Style & Presence: "It's giving main character energy."
  • Quality & Impression: "That trench coat is giving luxury."

Page 7: The Birthday Finale — Combining All 5 Creative Rules in COSY Manner 🍁

Before vs After Conversational Upgrade

  • Rigid Textbook B1 Version: "Let us meet around seven o'clock. I am enjoying this coffee shop very much. My coffee is having a stimulating effect today. We are eating lunch at our desks now. This cafe decor looks like a luxury resort."
  • Natural Expressive B2 Shakespearean Birthday Upgrade: "Let's meet at seven-ish if that works for you! I'm loving this autumn spot — the coffee is definitely coffee-ing today! While we're desk-lunching, notice how this whole setup is giving cozy luxury."

Mastering grammar isn't about avoiding mistakes: it is about adopting Shakespeare's creative courage to make your spoken and written voice memorable, natural, and uniquely yours!
