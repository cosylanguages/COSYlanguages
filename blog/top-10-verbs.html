01

🧍 To Be

The very first verb every beginner needs — and the one where languages disagree the most.

The trap: English uses to be for age, hunger and thirst (“I am 25”, “I am hungry”). Most other COSY languages don't. Watch what each language really does:

  • Age uses HAVE, not BE, in French, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese — « j'ai 25 ans », « ho 25 anni », « tengo 25 años », « tenho 25 anos » (literally “I have 25 years”).
  • Age uses BE in German and Greek, just like English — „Ich bin 25 Jahre alt“, « Είμαι 25 χρονών ».
  • Age uses neither in Russian: «Мне 25 лет» literally means “to-me 25 years” — there is no verb at all in the present tense sentence.
  • Hunger/thirst use HAVE in French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese and German — « j'ai faim », « ho fame », « tengo hambre », « tenho fome », „Ich habe Hunger“.
  • Greek has no BE or HAVE for hunger/thirst — it uses two single dedicated verbs: πεινάω (to-be-hungry) and διψάω (to-be-thirsty).
  • Russian usually drops “to be” entirely in the present tense — «Я Анна» (“I Anna”) is a complete, correct sentence.

Always double-check the age / hunger / thirst pattern per language before teaching it as a direct translation of English — it is the single biggest false-friend trap at A0.

02

🤝 To Have

Possession, family, and the moment several languages quietly swap in a different verb.

Russian barely uses this verb. The dictionary entry ru:imet:verb (иметь) is correct and formal, but in everyday Russian, possession is expressed with «у меня есть» (literally “at me there-is”) — e.g. «У меня есть машина», not «Я имею машину». Any phrase list for Russian beginners should teach the у меня есть construction first.

Body aches switch verbs. English and Spanish/Portuguese quietly swap HAVE for “hurt/doler/doer” (“my head hurts”), while French, Italian, German and Greek stay on HAVE-style constructions (« j'ai mal à la tête », „Ich habe Kopfschmerzen“).

🤔 To Think

Opinions — and the moment 'think' quietly becomes 'believe'.

“I think” often isn't the literal 'think' verb. For everyday opinions, Spanish prefers creer (to believe) over pensar, and Portuguese prefers achar (to find/consider) over pensar — both are more natural than a literal translation of “I think”. Greek similarly prefers νομίζω (to believe/reckon) for opinions, keeping σκέφτομαι (the COSYdata entry above) for the more literal 'to ponder/reflect'.

🧠 To Know

The classic two-verb trap: knowing a fact vs. knowing a person or place.

Two verbs for 'know' — and this is exactly the case the brief called out. French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese and German all split 'to know' into two separate verbs:

  • facts / how-to: savoir (fr) · sapere (it) · saber (es) · saber (pt) · wissen (de)
  • people / places: connaître (fr, missing from COSYdata) · conoscere (it) · conocer (es) · conhecer (pt) · kennen (de, missing from COSYdata)

Greek and Russian, by contrast, use a single verb (ξέρω / знать) for both meanings — no split needed. And for skills specifically, both French/Italian/Spanish/Portuguese use their facts verb (“I know how to swim” = savoir/sapere/saber nager/nuotare/nadar), while German switches to können (can) for the same idea — and Russian switches to a third verb, уметь (to know-how / master a skill), distinct from both знать (know facts) and мочь (can, general ability).

❤️ To Like

Half the languages here don't say 'I like it' — they say 'it pleases me'.

Subject-flip alert. In English, French, German and Portuguese, you are the subject who likes: “I like coffee.” But Italian, Spanish, Greek and Russian flip the sentence around — the thing becomes the subject and you become an indirect object, literally “coffee is-pleasing to-me”: mi piace il caffè · me gusta el café · μου αρέσει ο καφές · мне нравится кофе. This impersonal construction is one of the hardest A0 patterns to internalise for English speakers, and worth its own dedicated drill.

Also note: French aimer covers both “like” and “love” — « je t'aime » is romantic love, so “I like you” (platonically) needs the softened « je t'aime bien ».

🚶 To Go (+ 'going to...')

'Going to' isn't universal.
